What are the foundational theorems of statistical centrality and dispersion? Understanding how numbers behave mathematically around their averages reveals essential rules tested in FBISE examinations.

Theorem 1: Sum of Deviations from the Mean is Zero

The algebraic sum of deviations of a set of observations taken from their arithmetic mean ($\bar{x}$) is always identically equal to zero:

$$\sum (x - \bar{x}) = 0$$

Example: For data {2, 4, 6}, mean $\bar{x} = 4$, deviations: $(2-4) + (4-4) + (6-4) = -2 + 0 + 2 = 0$ ✓

Theorem 2: Variance of a Constant Dataset is Zero

If a dataset consists entirely of identical constant values (e.g., {4, 4, 4, 4, 4}), the data exhibits absolutely no spread or variation. Because every value matches the mean perfectly, the variance evaluates to exactly zero:

$$S^2 = 0$$

How do you compare consistency between different datasets?

Absolute standard deviations cannot be directly compared if datasets use different units or scales. The Coefficient of Variation ($CV$) is the definitive relative benchmark:

$$CV = \left(\frac{S}{\bar{x}}\right) \times 100\%$$

  • Higher CV = Greater relative volatility (less consistent)
  • Lower CV = More consistent data

What are data partition scales? Datasets are segmented into equal parts based on standard cut-off marks:

  • Quantiles/Quartiles: Divide data into 4 equal blocks (25% each)
  • Deciles: Divide data into 10 equal blocks (10% each)
  • Percentiles: Divide data into 100 equal parts (1% each)
