What is the primary role of the immune system? The immune system acts as a defense mechanism to protect the body against pathogens like bacteria, viruses, and fungi, as well as abnormal cells such as cancer cells.

What are innate and adaptive immunity?

  • Innate Immunity (Non-specific): Immediate response, present from birth. Includes physical barriers (skin, mucus), chemical barriers (stomach acid, enzymes), and cellular responses (phagocytes). Does not improve with repeated exposure.
  • Adaptive Immunity (Specific): Delayed but highly specific response. Develops after exposure to pathogens. Involves lymphocytes (B cells and T cells) and has memory (improves with repeated exposure).

What are the three lines of defense?

| Line | Type | Components | Specificity | |------|------|------------|-------------| | First line | Innate | Skin, mucous membranes, secretions (tears, saliva, stomach acid) | Non-specific | | Second line | Innate | Phagocytes (macrophages, neutrophils), inflammation, fever, antimicrobial proteins | Non-specific | | Third line | Adaptive | Lymphocytes (B cells, T cells), antibodies | Highly specific |

What are the main organs of the immune system?

  • Bone marrow: Produces all blood cells including B lymphocytes (B cells mature here).
  • Thymus: Gland where T lymphocytes (T cells) mature.
  • Lymph nodes: Small organs that filter lymph, trap pathogens, and house lymphocytes.
  • Spleen: Filters blood, removes old red blood cells, and contains lymphocytes.
  • Tonsils and adenoids: Trap pathogens entering through mouth and nose.

How does a coordinated immune response work?

  1. Antigen presentation: A macrophage (or dendritic cell) engulfs a pathogen and presents its antigen on MHC molecules.
  2. T-helper cell activation: T-helper cells (CD4+) recognize the antigen and become activated.
  3. B cell activation: Activated T-helper cells stimulate B cells to produce antibodies.
  4. Cytotoxic T cell activation: T-helper cells activate cytotoxic T cells (CD8+) to kill infected cells.
  5. Memory cell formation: Some B and T cells become memory cells for long-term protection.

How do memory cells ensure long-term protection? Memory B and T cells are created after an initial infection or vaccination. They "remember" the specific pathogen and initiate a faster and stronger response upon re-exposure (secondary immune response).

What are cytotoxic T cells? Cytotoxic T cells (CD8+ T cells) are specialized to recognize and kill virus-infected cells or tumor cells directly.

How do vaccines and booster doses work?

  • Vaccines: Train the immune system to produce antibodies without causing illness. They contain weakened, inactivated, or parts of a pathogen.
  • Booster doses: Strengthen immunity by increasing the population of memory cells and targeting new virus variants. Needed for some vaccines (e.g., COVID-19, tetanus).

What is the difference between primary and secondary immune responses?

| Feature | Primary Response | Secondary Response | |---------|-----------------|-------------------| | Timing | Slow (days to weeks) | Fast (hours to days) | | Magnitude | Lower antibody levels | Higher antibody levels | | Memory | None initially | Strong (memory cells) | | Example | First exposure to pathogen | Re-exposure to same pathogen |
