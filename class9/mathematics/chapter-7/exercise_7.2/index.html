What is the midpoint formula? The midpoint formula is used to find the exact center point of a line segment connecting two points.

  • Formula: $M = \left(\frac{x_1 + x_2}{2}, \frac{y_1 + y_2}{2}\right)$
  • The midpoint is the average of the x-coordinates and the average of the y-coordinates

How do we find the midpoint between two points? Substitute the coordinates into the formula and simplify.

  • Example: Find the midpoint of $A(2,5)$ and $B(6,9)$
  • $M = \left(\frac{2 + 6}{2}, \frac{5 + 9}{2}\right) = \left(\frac{8}{2}, \frac{14}{2}\right) = (4,7)$

How do we verify if diagonals bisect each other in a quadrilateral? In any quadrilateral, the diagonals bisect each other if and only if the shape is a parallelogram.

  • Step 1: Find the midpoint of diagonal $PR$ (from vertex P to vertex R)
  • Step 2: Find the midpoint of diagonal $QS$ (from vertex Q to vertex S)
  • Step 3: If both midpoints are the same point, the diagonals bisect each other
  • Important: Check both x and y coordinates — if even one differs, they do NOT bisect

Example - Verifying diagonal bisection: For a quadrilateral with vertices $P(1,2)$, $Q(3,4)$, $R(5,6)$, $S(3,4)$:

  • Midpoint of $PR$ = $((1+5)/2, (2+6)/2) = (6/2, 8/2) = (3,4)$
  • Midpoint of $QS$ = $((3+3)/2, (4+4)/2) = (6/2, 8/2) = (3,4)$
  • Since midpoints are equal, diagonals bisect each other ✓

What is the Midpoint Theorem? The Midpoint Theorem states that the segment joining the midpoints of any two sides of a triangle is parallel to the third side and is half its length.

  • Given: Triangle $ABC$ with $D$ as midpoint of $AB$ and $E$ as midpoint of $AC$
  • To prove: $DE = \frac{1}{2}BC$
  • Method: Find coordinates of $D$ and $E$ using midpoint formula, then use distance formula to calculate $DE$ and $BC$

Example - Proving Midpoint Theorem: In triangle $A(0,0)$, $B(4,0)$, $C(2,4)$:

  • Midpoint $D$ of $AB = ((0+4)/2, (0+0)/2) = (2,0)$
  • Midpoint $E$ of $AC = ((0+2)/2, (0+4)/2) = (1,2)$
  • $DE = \sqrt{(2-1)^2 + (0-2)^2} = \sqrt{1 + 4} = \sqrt{5}$
  • $BC = \sqrt{(4-2)^2 + (0-4)^2} = \sqrt{4 + 16} = \sqrt{20} = 2\sqrt{5}$
  • Since $DE = \frac{1}{2}BC$, the theorem is verified ✓

How do we use trigonometric components to find target coordinates? When moving at an angle, use sine and cosine to find the components.

  • Horizontal component (x-direction): $x = r \cos \theta$
  • Vertical component (y-direction): $y = r \sin \theta$
  • Example: A point moves 6 units at $45^\circ$ from the origin → $x = 6 \times \cos 45^\circ = 6 \times \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} = 3\sqrt{2} \approx 4.24$, $y = 6 \times \sin 45^\circ = 3\sqrt{2} \approx 4.24$ → target coordinates $(3\sqrt{2}, 3\sqrt{2})$

How do we find missing vertices of geometric shapes? Using known properties of squares and rectangles:

  • Square: All sides equal, all angles $90^\circ$, diagonals equal
  • Rectangle: Opposite sides equal, all angles $90^\circ$, diagonals equal
  • Given three vertices, the fourth can be found using midpoint of diagonals (diagonals bisect each other)
  • Given two adjacent vertices, use side lengths and perpendicular directions

Example - Finding missing vertex of a square: Three vertices of a square are $A(1,1)$, $B(4,1)$, $C(4,4)$. Find $D$.

  • Observe: $A$ to $B$ is horizontal (right 3 units)
  • $B$ to $C$ is vertical (up 3 units)
  • Therefore $A$ to $D$ should be vertical (up 3 units): $D = (1, 1+3) = (1,4)$
  • Verify $C$ to $D$ is horizontal (left 3 units): $(4-3,4) = (1,4)$ ✓

Important note for diagonal bisection: When checking if diagonals bisect each other, verify BOTH coordinates match. If $M_{diag1} = (3,4)$ and $M_{diag2} = (3,4.5)$, they DO NOT bisect.
