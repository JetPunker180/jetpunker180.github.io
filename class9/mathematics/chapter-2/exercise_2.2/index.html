What is a logarithm? Exercise 2.2, "Logarithmic and Exponential Forms," explores the inverse relationship between powers and logarithms. A logarithm answers the question: "To what exponent must the base be raised to get the argument?"

When is a logarithmic expression defined? A logarithm $\log_a x$ is only defined when:

  • Base $a > 0$ (positive)
  • Base $a \neq 1$ (not equal to 1)
  • Argument $x > 0$ (positive)

Important: $\log_1(argument)$ and $\log_0(argument)$ are undefined. Also, $\log_1 = 0$ is incorrect notation.

What is the relationship between exponential and logarithmic forms? You will master the fundamental skill of interconverting between forms:

  • Exponential form: $x = a^y$
  • Logarithmic form: $\log_a x = y$
  • Key relationship: $x = a^y \iff \log_a x = y$

Examples of conversion:

  • $2^3 = 8$ in exponential form becomes $\log_2 8 = 3$ in logarithmic form
  • $\log_5 25 = 2$ in logarithmic form becomes $5^2 = 25$ in exponential form
  • $\log_6 1 = 0$ (since $6^0 = 1$)

How do we solve for unknown variables in logarithmic equations? A significant portion of the exercise is dedicated to solving for unknown variables ($x$, $y$, or $b$):

  • Step 1: Convert the logarithmic equation to its exponential equivalent
  • Step 2: Use algebraic techniques (equating bases, applying fractional exponents)
  • Step 3: Solve for the unknown variable

Example - Solving by equating bases: $2^{x+1} = 2^6 \implies x+1 = 6 \implies x = 5$

Example - Solving a logarithmic equation: $\log_2 (x+3) = 4$ converts to $2^4 = x+3 \implies 16 = x+3 \implies x = 13$

Special case: When the argument of a logarithm is 1, the result is always 0 regardless of the valid base (e.g., $\log_6(1) = 0$) because $a^0 = 1$ for any valid base $a$.
