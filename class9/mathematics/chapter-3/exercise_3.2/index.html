What are word problems on set operations? Exercise 3.2, "Word Problems on Set Operations," applies set theory to solve complex real-world scenarios through the Principle of Inclusion-Exclusion (PIE).

What is the Principle of Inclusion-Exclusion for two sets? The fundamental formula for two sets is:

  • $n(A \cup B) = n(A) + n(B) - n(A \cap B)$
  • Used to find unknown intersections or unions in groups like language learners or sports players

How do we calculate 'only' categories? You will learn to calculate specific subsets such as:

  • Only A: $n(\text{only }A) = n(A) - n(A \cap B)$
  • Only B: $n(\text{only }B) = n(B) - n(A \cap B)$
  • Only A or only B: $n(\text{only }A \cup \text{only }B) = n(A) + n(B) - 2n(A \cap B)$

How do we find the 'neither' category (complements)? To find individuals who belong to neither group:

  • $n(\text{neither}) = n(U) - n(A \cup B)$
  • Where $n(U)$ is the total universal set population
  • This subtracts everyone in A or B from total population

What is the Principle of Inclusion-Exclusion for three sets? The extended formula for three sets accounts for triple intersections:

  • $n(A \cup B \cup C) = n(A) + n(B) + n(C) - n(A \cap B) - n(A \cap C) - n(B \cap C) + n(A \cap B \cap C)$

Why do we add back the triple intersection? When we subtract all pairwise intersections, the elements in $A \cap B \cap C$ have been subtracted too many times (once for each of the three pairs), so we add back the triple intersection once to correct the count.

How do we find 'only one set' in three-set problems?

  • $n(\text{only }A) = n(A) - n(A \cap B) - n(A \cap C) + n(A \cap B \cap C)$
  • $n(\text{only }B) = n(B) - n(A \cap B) - n(B \cap C) + n(A \cap B \cap C)$
  • $n(\text{only }C) = n(C) - n(A \cap C) - n(B \cap C) + n(A \cap B \cap C)$

Example - Two set problem: In a class of 50 students, 30 like Math, 25 like Science, and 15 like both. Find how many like neither.

  • $n(M \cup S) = 30 + 25 - 15 = 40$
  • $n(\text{neither}) = 50 - 40 = 10$ students

Example - Three set problem: In a survey of 100 people, 50 like tea (T), 40 like coffee (C), 30 like milk (M), 20 like T and C, 15 like T and M, 10 like C and M, and 5 like all three. Find how many like exactly one drink.

  • $n(T \cup C \cup M) = 50 + 40 + 30 - 20 - 15 - 10 + 5 = 80$
  • $n(\text{only }T) = 50 - 20 - 15 + 5 = 20$
  • $n(\text{only }C) = 40 - 20 - 10 + 5 = 15$
  • $n(\text{only }M) = 30 - 15 - 10 + 5 = 10$
  • $n(\text{exactly one}) = 20 + 15 + 10 = 45$ people

Common mistake to avoid: Forgetting to add back the triple intersection $n(A \cap B \cap C)$ in three-set problems will give an incorrect result that is too low.

Key applications covered:

  • Student group surveys (sports, languages, subjects)
  • House features surveys (car porches, garages, gardens)
  • Customer preferences (product choices, TV channels)
  • Technology usage surveys
