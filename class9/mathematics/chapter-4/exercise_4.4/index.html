What are HCF and LCM of polynomials? Exercise 4.4, "HCF and LCM of Polynomials," introduces advanced systematic approaches for finding common factors and multiples of algebraic expressions.

How do we find HCF using the division method? The division method is vital for polynomials where standard factorization is complex:

  • Divide the polynomial of higher degree by the one of lower degree
  • If remainder is zero, divisor is the HCF
  • If remainder is not zero, divide the divisor by the remainder
  • Repeat until remainder is zero (Euclidean algorithm)
  • Last non-zero divisor is the HCF
  • May require rearrangement of terms (e.g., write in descending powers of variable)
  • May require multiplying by suitable numbers to make division work

Example - Division method for HCF: Find HCF of $x^3 - 3x^2 - x + 3$ and $x^2 + 2x - 3$

  • Divide $x^3 - 3x^2 - x + 3$ by $x^2 + 2x - 3$
  • Quotient = $x - 5$, Remainder = $12x - 12 = 12(x - 1)$
  • Now divide $x^2 + 2x - 3$ by $x - 1$
  • $x^2 + 2x - 3 = (x - 1)(x + 3)$
  • Remainder = 0, so HCF = $x - 1$

What is the LCM of monomials using tabular method? Create a table with:

  • Row for numerical coefficients (take LCM of numbers)
  • Row for each variable ($x$, $y$, $z$, etc.) - for each variable, take the highest power present
  • Multiply all together

Example - LCM of monomials: Find LCM of $6x^3y^2$, $8x^2y^3$, and $12xy^4$

  • Numerical: LCM of 6, 8, 12 = 24
  • $x$: highest power = $x^3$
  • $y$: highest power = $y^4$
  • LCM = $24x^3y^4$

How do we find LCM of polynomials by factorization? Method:

  • Step 1: Factorize each polynomial completely using algebraic identities
  • Step 2: Identify all distinct factors present
  • Step 3: For each factor, take the highest power that appears in any polynomial
  • Step 4: Multiply these factors to get LCM

What algebraic identities are used for LCM factorization?

  • Difference of squares: $a^2 - b^2 = (a + b)(a - b)$
  • Sum of cubes: $a^3 + b^3 = (a + b)(a^2 - ab + b^2)$
  • Difference of cubes: $a^3 - b^3 = (a - b)(a^2 + ab + b^2)$
  • Square of sum/difference: $(a \pm b)^2 = a^2 \pm 2ab + b^2$
  • Middle-term breaking for quadratic trinomials

Why is full simplification important? Before calculating LCM, fully simplify all factors:

  • Example: $a^6 - 1 = (a^3 - 1)(a^3 + 1)$
  • Further factor: $a^3 - 1 = (a - 1)(a^2 + a + 1)$
  • Further factor: $a^3 + 1 = (a + 1)(a^2 - a + 1)$
  • So $a^6 - 1 = (a - 1)(a + 1)(a^2 + a + 1)(a^2 - a + 1)$

What are the properties of HCF?

  • Constant multipliers do NOT change HCF: HCF of $p(x)$ and $3p(x)$ is $p(x)$ (not $3p(x)$)
  • Variable multipliers DO change HCF: HCF of $p(x)$ and $x \cdot p(x)$ is $p(x)$? Actually need to check common factors
  • Example: HCF of $x(x+1)$ and $(x+1)$ is $(x+1)$

Example - LCM of polynomials by factorization: Find LCM of $x^2 - 1$, $x^2 + 2x + 1$, and $x^3 - 1$

  • $x^2 - 1 = (x - 1)(x + 1)$
  • $x^2 + 2x + 1 = (x + 1)^2$
  • $x^3 - 1 = (x - 1)(x^2 + x + 1)$
  • Distinct factors: $(x - 1)$, $(x + 1)$, $(x^2 + x + 1)$
  • Highest powers: $(x - 1)^1$, $(x + 1)^2$, $(x^2 + x + 1)^1$
  • LCM = $(x - 1)(x + 1)^2(x^2 + x + 1)$

Key differences between HCF and LCM:

  • HCF: Takes smallest powers of common factors
  • LCM: Takes highest powers of all distinct factors
  • For any two polynomials: Product of polynomials = HCF × LCM (if no extra constants)

Common mistakes to avoid:

  • Forgetting to fully factorize before LCM (e.g., leaving $a^6 - 1$ as is)
  • Using HCF method for LCM or vice versa
  • Not rearranging terms before division method
  • Forgetting to multiply by suitable numbers when needed in division
