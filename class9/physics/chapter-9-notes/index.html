Chapter 9, "Nature of Physics and Scientific Methodology," explores the foundational principles that guide physical inquiry and the broad reach of the discipline. This chapter answers fundamental questions about what physics is, how it works, and why it matters. What are the branches of physics? You will learn about various branches of physics: - Classical Mechanics: Study of motion of macroscopic objects (cars, planets, projectiles). Domains: Newton's laws, kinematics, dynamics, rotational motion. - Thermodynamics: Study of heat, temperature, and energy transfer. Examples: engines, refrigerators, air conditioners. - Optics: Study of light and its behavior (reflection, refraction, diffraction, interference). Examples: lenses, microscopes, telescopes, glasses. - Electromagnetism: Study of electric and magnetic fields and their interactions. Examples: motors, generators, transformers, radio waves. - Nuclear Physics: Study of atomic nucleus (protons, neutrons, radioactivity, nuclear reactions). Examples: nuclear power plants, PET scans. - Particle Physics: Study of fundamental particles (quarks, leptons, bosons) and their interactions. - Astrophysics: Application of physics to celestial objects (stars, galaxies, black holes,宇宙). Examples: understanding stellar evolution, galaxy formation. - Biophysics: Application of physics principles to biological systems. Examples: medical imaging (MRI, CT), biomechanics. - Geophysics: Study of Earth using physics principles (seismic waves, Earth's magnetic field, gravity). - Acoustics: Study of sound waves and their properties. Examples: ultrasound, noise control, musical instruments. - Computational Physics: Use of computers to solve physics problems and simulate physical systems. What is the difference between classical and quantum mechanics? You will distinguish between: - Classical Mechanics: Deals with macroscopic objects (large scale, everyday sizes). Objects have definite positions and velocities. Deterministic (predictable). Examples: motion of planets, cars, baseballs, pendulums. - Quantum Mechanics: Deals with microscopic particles (atomic and subatomic scale). Particles behave as both particles and waves (wave-particle duality). Probabilistic (uncertainty principle). Examples: electron behavior in atoms, photon emission, quantum computing. What is the scientific method? A major focus is placed on the scientific method: - Observation: Noticing a phenomenon or problem using senses or instruments. - Question: Asking why or how the phenomenon occurs. - Hypothesis: A tentative explanation or educated guess that can be tested through experimentation. Must be falsifiable (can be proven wrong). - Prediction: Logical consequences of the hypothesis (If... then... statements). - Experimentation: Controlled testing of the hypothesis. Collecting data. - Analysis: Interpreting results to determine if hypothesis is supported. - Conclusion: Drawing conclusions and communicating results. What is the difference between a hypothesis, theory, and law? You will learn: - Hypothesis: A tentative explanation for a phenomenon. Based on limited evidence. Testable through experiments. Can be modified or rejected if not supported. - Theory: A comprehensive set of ideas that explains WHY phenomena occur. Well-substantiated by a large body of evidence. Predictive (can make testable predictions). Can be modified as new evidence emerges. NOT "just a guess" - in science, theory represents our deepest understanding. Examples: Theory of Evolution, Theory of Relativity, Quantum Theory, Germ Theory of Disease. - Law: A concise statement that describes WHAT happens in nature under specific conditions. Often expressed as a mathematical equation. Universally valid within its domain. Does NOT explain why. Examples: Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation, Law of Conservation of Energy, Ohm's Law, Boyle's Law. - Key Distinction: Laws describe what happens; theories explain why it happens. What are the differences between science, technology, and engineering? You will understand: - Science: Pursuit of knowledge about the natural world. Primary goal: understanding (knowledge generation). Process: scientific method (observation, hypothesis, experimentation). Output: laws, theories, discoveries. Question driven: "Why?" - Technology: Application of scientific knowledge for practical purposes. Primary goal: useful tools and products. Process: invention, design, innovation. Output: devices, systems, processes. Problem driven: "How can we use this?" - Engineering: Design and building of structures, machines, and systems. Primary goal: solving practical problems within constraints (cost, safety, materials). Process: engineering design cycle (identify problem, design, prototype, test, improve). Output: buildings, bridges, circuits, software. Constraint driven: "How can we build this?" - Interrelationship: Science generates knowledge → Technology applies that knowledge → Engineering builds practical solutions → New tools enable new scientific discoveries Why is physics important in daily life, medicine, and technology? The chapter highlights: - Daily Life: Electricity (lights, fans, appliances), mobile phones, computers, internet, television, radio, transportation (cars, trains, airplanes), GPS navigation, cameras, microwaves, refrigerators, air conditioners. - Medicine: X-rays (electromagnetism), MRI (magnetic resonance imaging), CT scans (computed tomography), ultrasound (acoustics), radiation therapy (nuclear physics), laser surgery (optics), pacemakers (electricity), prosthetics (mechanics). - Technology: Satellites (orbital mechanics, electromagnetism), GPS (relativity, electromagnetism), computers (quantum mechanics, electronics), internet (fiber optics, electromagnetism), renewable energy (solar panels, wind turbines), electric vehicles. What is the role of physics as a foundational science? Physics is the most fundamental science because it studies matter, energy, space, and time at the most basic level. It provides the foundation for: - Chemistry: Chemical bonding, atomic structure, reaction rates, thermodynamics - Biology: Biophysics, biomechanics, membrane transport, nerve impulses, photosynthesis - Earth Science: Seismology (earthquakes), meteorology (weather), oceanography (waves, currents) - Astronomy: Stellar evolution, planetary motion, cosmology These notes are strictly aligned with the Student Learning Outcomes (SLOs) for the FBISE 2026 annual examination. # SLOs (Student Learning Outcomes - Written as questions) chapter-first-slo: "What are the branches of physics and the difference between classical and quantum mechanics?
